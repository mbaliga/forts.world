# Janjira campaign, 1733 — starter research note

Status: **supported at campaign-outline level; insufficient for historical production**

This note records what has been reviewed so far and prevents prototype inventions from hardening into supposed fact.

## Findings currently supported

The principal reviewed synthesis is V. G. Dighe's *Peshwa Bajirao I and Maratha Expansion* (1944), especially its three Janjira chapters. It uses published Peshwa records, Marathi collections, English factory correspondence, Portuguese records, and other material available to the author. It is a strong starting point, but its age, framing, OCR quality, and interpretations require triangulation with primary records and modern scholarship.

- In 1733 Bajirao established himself at Rajpuri for operations against Janjira.
- The proposed reduction of the island fort depended on cooperation with the Angre navy and a combined land-and-sea attack.
- Dighe describes the fort at the mouth of Rajpuri creek, separated from the mainland and protected by high walls, bastions, artillery, and water approaches that made a direct attack exceptionally difficult.
- Early-monsoon Arabian Sea conditions made the use of small craft dangerous and contributed to Sekhoji Angre's refusal to risk his fleet at the expected time.
- The Sidi garrison continued to receive provisions when water approaches were not controlled. Bajirao concluded that an effective blockade had to work by land and sea and that his available force was inadequate without naval support and more resources.
- Mainland and coastal operations involved places beyond the island fort; supply points, outposts, ports, other forts, and hostile or competing forces mattered to the wider campaign.
- Sekhoji Angre's death disrupted the planned post-monsoon attack. Command relations and the failure to secure sustained naval cooperation contributed to the offensive ending without the capture of Janjira.

Source: [V. G. Dighe, *Peshwa Bajirao I and Maratha Expansion* (1944), scanned text](https://ia803207.us.archive.org/21/items/in.gov.ignca.4317/4317_text.pdf), particularly pp. 48–81 in the printed pagination. Source ID: `JAN-001`.

## Safe gameplay implications

These are design deductions from the supported outline, not claims that the prototype simulates exact events:

- Make fleet cooperation, command relationships, and timing first-class strategic constraints.
- Make Rajpuri and other mainland positions matter to supply, observation, artillery, and access.
- Model blockade as control of multiple ingress/egress routes rather than parking one boat on a marker.
- Make monsoon conditions alter sea access, movement risk, roads, forage, supply, and the campaign window.
- Allow political and command failure to prevent an apparently straightforward military plan.
- Permit partial territorial or diplomatic outcomes rather than requiring capture of the island fort as the only victory state.

## Prototype elements that are not historical claims

- The 24×16 generated map and positions of every anchor.
- The ten-minute-scale monsoon timer.
- Starting resources, production rates, costs, damage, speed, range, and health values.
- The number, type, equipment, and placement of units and craft.
- The use and spelling of `galivat` in the data file pending vessel research.
- The representation of the Sidi faction, colours, guards, and patrol craft.
- The scout marker, blockade marker, capturable outpost, and requirement to hold 60 supply.
- Any implication that Bajirao personally directed every local action or that Janjira was captured.

## Research gaps before production

- Day-by-day chronology and command structure.
- Reliable 1733 operational map, historical coastline, roads, settlements, ports, batteries, water depths, channels, and seasonal access.
- Measured fort plan and construction phases; separation of eighteenth-century fabric from later alteration.
- Orders of battle, recruitment, garrisons, casualties, artillery, ammunition, uniforms/clothing, flags, camp organization, and medical/logistical practice.
- Angre and Sidi vessel types, dimensions, rigs/oars, carrying capacity, weapons, crew, seaworthiness, and tactics.
- English and Portuguese involvement, goals, deployments, and records.
- Local civilian communities, economy, food, water, trade, language, and consequences of the campaign.
- Marathi/Persian/Portuguese terminology and pronunciation.
- Modern scholarship and review by historians of Maratha, Sidi, Konkan, and maritime history.

## Representation guardrail

The campaign should not be written as a simple civilizational contest or as the story of one flawless hero. It involves the Maratha court, Bajirao, multiple Maratha office-holders, the Angres, the Siddis of Janjira, local communities, English and Portuguese interests, competing commands, logistics, weather, and negotiation. The game needs perspectives and consequences that reflect that complexity.

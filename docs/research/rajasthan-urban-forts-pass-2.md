# Rajasthan urban forts: aerial and documentary review, pass 2

This is a research log for five playable prototypes, not a certification of faithful reconstructions. The five scenes remain **improved geometry; not verified**. Their catalogue coordinates are site markers; they are not treated as surveyed wall locations.

## Site-by-site findings

### Jaisalmer

The existing custom scene has the richest geometry in this group: a Trikuta hill, multiple defensive rings, an indirect ascending gate sequence, dense inhabited lanes, palace and temple districts, and wells named by the Government-submitted UNESCO Site Management Plan. A current satellite view confirms the broad present relationship: the compact citadel rises amid a dense urban grid, with newer city fabric extending beyond it. The view is useful for context only; its image acquisition date is not provided, and no coordinate registration was done in this pass. The building lots, slope, exact bastion positions, waterworks and historic phases still need alignment to the Site Management Plan, conservation drawings and dated photographs.

### Mehrangarh and Jodhpur

The custom scene already models an elevated rocky ridge, a long defended approach, gates, palace courts and ramps. The aerial check confirms the broad urban relation: the fort sits on exposed high ground west of dense Jodhpur, with Jaswant Thada to its north and the historic city spreading to its east and south. The existing Ranisar and Padmasar shapes remain approximate contextual cues; neither their shorelines nor historical water levels were measured. The fort geometry is still not tied to a DEM or orthophoto. Mehrangarh Museum Trust trail descriptions support named features and connections such as Jai Pol, Fateh Pol, Chokelao Bagh and Rani Sagar, but views and routes are not equivalent to a measured plan.

### Junagarh, Bikaner

Rajasthan Tourism dates the fort to 1588 under Raja Rai Singh. The current aerial shows a long, rectilinear lowland enclosure with a substantial palace complex and immediate dense city blocks; Soorsagar Lake lies farther north. A subsequent official-measurement pass replaces the earlier visual-only footprint estimate with an area/perimeter-derived rectangle. The wall trace, gate bearings, bastions, palace positions and neighborhood blocks remain schematic. The aerial was used only for a broad north–south orientation check; it has not been registered or traced. Current city blocks and modern roads are not represented as a measured 1605 plan.

### Lohagarh, Bharatpur

The current aerial shows a moat-enclosed, angled and projecting fort footprint embedded in Bharatpur’s dense street fabric. The scene perimeter was enlarged and rotated to better match that broad visible extent. The ASI inventory accessed through IGNCA describes a rectangular enclosure, eight bastions and a moat of nominal width; Rajasthan Tourism names Ashtadhatu gate, Chowburja gate, the palaces and the two victory towers. The map check does not settle the precise perimeter, internal plan, current moat water or historic water management. Those need a traced ASI plan and dated conservation records.

### Bhatner, Hanumangarh

The ASI protected-monument register supplies the site coordinates and its inventory provides a scaled site plan; Rajasthan’s district and tourism sources describe the fort’s Ghaggar setting and the 1805 capture and renaming. The current aerial shows the fort as a distinct low enclosure amid Hanumangarh’s rectilinear streets, with agricultural plots close to its northern edge. The scene’s plan-shaped footprint was enlarged to match the rough visible scale, but its vertices and twelve-per-side bastion spacing remain undigitized. I removed the previous guessed blue river channel because the current aerial does not support that placement. The river relationship remains a documented historical fact, while its present channel and historical course require hydrological and map research.

## Evidence and reconstruction status

Official tourism and ASI/UNESCO sources support historical names, some dates and descriptions, and (for Bhatner) a plan with scale. Current aerial imagery was visually inspected for broad site shape and settlement relationships. Google Maps displayed current imagery attribution during review, but the acquisition dates of those image tiles were not shown. No aerial measurements are labeled as surveyed, no site plans have been orthorectified, no building footprint was traced from the screen, and no image has been republished as a game texture.

The models still need, site by site: digitized and georeferenced fort boundaries; DEM and water/terrain alignment; footprint-level palace, gate, temple and settlement plans; dated architectural phases tied to documented owners; historical roads and water systems; archival-view matching; and a condition/date record for every inferred feature. Uncertain elements should remain visibly identified as inferred or unknown in the game.

## Junagarh dimensions and architectural phases: 31 October 2026

The Government of India tourism page reports an enclosed area of 63,119 square yards and describes a rectangular layout. The 1951 Bikaner District Census Handbook states a circumference of 1,078 yards and a moat; CCRT’s Ministry of Culture Rajasthan-forts volume describes the outer wall as approximately 990 m in circumference. Combining the handbook perimeter (985.5 m after unit conversion) with the tourism-page area (about 52,779 m²) gives a working rectangle of approximately 339 × 156 m. This is a mathematical fit to two published aggregate measures, not a surveyed footprint; the small discrepancy with CCRT’s rounded perimeter is retained. The long-axis orientation is a broad visual comparison with the current aerial. No wall vertex, gate bearing, moat edge or bastion location has been digitized.

The 1951 handbook assigns Phool Mahal, Chandra Mahal and Gajmandir to Gaj Singh and Anup Mahal to Surat Singh. The model therefore withholds these named buildings from the 1594 completion snapshot, introduces the Gaj Singh group in a c.1780 illustrative state, and introduces Anup Mahal in the Surat Singh state. The 1951 record does not give exact years for these palace additions; Rajasthan Tourism independently records Gaj Singh founding Gajner Palace in 1784, which places this Bikaner ruler in the late eighteenth century but does not date the Junagarh palaces. The Government tourism page identifies Ganga Mahal as a twentieth-century addition, so it appears only in the present state. Ruler associations do not demonstrate that present-day fabric existed unchanged; all building footprints and positions remain schematic. Sources disagree slightly on the fort-completion chronology: Rajasthan Tourism and the Ministry of Culture give 1588–1593, while Incredible India describes a 1589 foundation and 1594 completion. The scene uses 1594 as its completed-state cutoff and exposes the disagreement.

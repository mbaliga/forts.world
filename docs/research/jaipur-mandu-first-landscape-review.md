# First landscape review: Jaipur–Amer and Mandu

Status: evidence-led first pass, **not a completed architectural survey**. Current game scenes remain prototypes. The research establishes what the existing scenes fail to include and which evidence should drive the next reconstruction. Catalogue points remain approximate.

## Amer, Jaigarh and the Amber valley

The UNESCO nomination describes Amber in the Kalikho Hills valley, below and connected to Jaigarh. Its wider buffer includes parts of Nahargarh Wildlife Sanctuary and the entire town of Amber in the valley. A UNESCO evaluation further describes the shared defensive landscape as including Jaigarh, Amber Palace, Amber village, Lake Maota and remaining fortification walls. Rajasthan Tourism describes the palace complex as four principal courtyards, approached through Suraj Pol and Chand Pol into Jaleb Chowk, with royal, residential and religious buildings. Jaipur Development Authority's regional base map includes contours, roads and watercourses; the JDA attraction page identifies Amer as an earlier capital and Jaigarh as an artillery centre.

The 2023 Rajasthan Forest Department/JDA eco-sensitive-zone report explicitly treats Amer, Panna Meena Kund, Sagar Lake, Jaigarh, Nahargarh, Gatore ki Chhatriyan and Jal Mahal as one historical tourism circuit. Its described order is Amer Fort → Panna Meena Kund → Sagar Lake → Jaigarh → Nahargarh → Gatore → Jal Mahal, with an optional foot route down from Nahargarh. This is good evidence for the regional campaign connection and site sequence, but the narrative is not a surveyed route polyline; each road, path, pass, seasonal constraint and fort entrance still needs georeferencing. The same report describes the sanctuary as hilly with narrow valleys and elevations from roughly 400 to 648 m, which should constrain the shared terrain surface rather than three independent invented hills. Source: [Rajasthan Forest Department/JDA, Zonal Master Plan for Nahargarh ESZ, §§1.5.3 and 17.4 (2023)](https://www.tourism.rajasthan.gov.in/content/dam/environment/Env/PDF_Files_II/Final%20Report%20Tourism%20Matserplan%20Nahargarh%20ESZ%2003-07-2023.pdf).

The current Amer and Jaigarh scenes remain separate procedural hill maps. Amer has named courtyard and palace cues, Maota Lake and two named gates; Jaigarh has named palace cues, a cannon and reservoir. Jaigarh's former sub-kilometre scene is now scaled to the Jaipur Municipal Corporation's reported 3 km north–south by 1 km dimensions, but the resulting ellipse is only a size/orientation cue, not a traced perimeter. Jaigarh exploration starts at an interior origin so its POIs remain reachable; that is a gameplay start point, not a documented historic entrance. Neither scene yet has registered shared walls, the ridge corridor, approach routes, Amber settlement, or current surrounding fabric. They still need to become one navigable landscape with separate site identities and evidence-aware period states.

The Amer timeline now separates a c. 1610 state from a c. 1640 state: the ICOMOS/UNESCO evaluation dates the fourth-court Man Singh Mahal to 1589–1614 and the second- and third-court groups to 1622–1667. The game withholds its schematic Diwan-i-Aam/Diwan-i-Khas group from the earlier snapshot and retains it in the present-day state. This repairs a dated-phase error; the structure locations and footprints are still not traced.

Evidence supports the relationship and landscape, but does not establish complete fort footprints. A Government of India forest-clearance map supplies 20 GPS points for the Amber Museum & Palace application area and labels Jaigarh, walls, nearby settlements, roads and temples; its polygon is shown only as present-day site context, not as the fort wall or as an earlier-period boundary. The map reports 22.5 ha, while a local projection gives about 22.2 ha; retain both values until the source GIS resolves the difference. The 2023 JDA/Forest Department report adds satellite sheets with separate existing-site and existing-structure outlines for Amer (fig. 13.9, 200 m scale) and Jaigarh (fig. 13.17, 260 m scale). Their yellow structure envelopes are now manually traced as present-day overlays, while the red site outlines remain distinct from fort walls. The same report shows the Nahargarh sheet (fig. 13.13, 120 m scale). Capture dates for these satellite images are not stated, so they are mapped references rather than verified current aerials. Jaipur foothill house clusters are visual context only, not registered building footprints. The UNESCO nomination boundary/map and JDA contour/water map still need georeferencing; the exact subterranean route and its construction chronology remain uncertain.

The playable Amer map marker now uses the UNESCO nomination's site reference coordinate, N 26°59′05″, E 75°51′07″ (26.9847222, 75.8519444), instead of the approximate catalogue point 26.985, 75.851. This moves the discovery marker about 100 m southeast. It does not move or validate the local 3D layout: the coordinate is a site reference, not a wall vertex, and the local terrain origin remains separate. The UNESCO dossier also lists the Amer property at 30 ha and buffer at 498 ha; these mapped extents remain distinct from both the 20-point applied-land polygon and the fort wall. Source: [UNESCO Hill Forts of Rajasthan nomination](https://whc.unesco.org/uploads/nominations/247rev.pdf), Amber Fort entry and Maps 1.12, 1.18 and 1.24.

## Jaipur's walled city as the campaign settlement

Jaipur is a separate, connected settlement context, not another fort to add to the original 138. UNESCO documents a planned 18th-century grid, chaupars, chowkris, walls and gates, bazaar fronts, craft streets, havelis, temples, homes and current living uses. JDA publishes a Wall City map and regional planning maps. The game currently has no connected Jaipur urban landscape. A later regional layer should place the walled city and its gates, streets, markets and public spaces in their real relation to Amber, Jaigarh and Nahargarh; it should not collapse the whole metropolis into a circular fort map.

## Nahargarh

Rajasthan Tourism dates construction to 1734 and the Madhavendra Bhawan expansion to 1868. Its current official page describes twelve matching two-storey queen's boudoirs and a king's suite; the 2023 JDA/Forest Department report says nine apartments. These accounts may count different architectural units, so the discrepancy remains unresolved. The model retains twelve repeated blocks only as diagrammatic placeholders; their count, positions, dimensions and corridors are not measured geometry. The 2023 satellite sheet's existing-structure envelope is represented as a present-day overlay, and a small cluster of west-foothill houses cues the current urban edge. These traces do not establish room-by-room geometry or cadastral settlement footprints. The Government of India forest-clearance portal supplies 15 GPS points for the required forest-land application area named “Nahargarh Fort with Wall”; these are shown only as a present-day administrative site boundary, not as the historic fort wall. The source calculates 21.255 ha as 2,125.5 m × 100 m, while a local projection of the listed vertices encloses about 19.5 ha; clarify the discrepancy and the relationship between application area, mapped existing site and wall alignment. The 1734 and 1868 states are distinct, but fort walls, palace plan, ridge, routes and Jaipur's changing urban edge remain untraced against dated imagery.

The government's 2022 forest-clearance plan distinguishes the “Nahargarh Fort with Wall” polygon from the forest-land boundary. Its 2,125.5 m / 100 m / 21.255 ha table describes an applied forest segment and its area calculation, not a surveyed fort-wall perimeter; those values must not be used as the wall circuit or fort dimensions. Rajasthan Tourism's 2023 ESZ report also contains satellite-style existing-site maps for Nahargarh, Amer and Jaigarh, including north arrows and scale bars. Their imagery capture dates are not stated in the report text, so they are useful plan references but cannot yet be represented as a verified current-aerial comparison.

## Mandu's fortified plateau and inhabited landscape

The Archaeological Survey of India-submitted UNESCO tentative-list entry identifies 61 protected monuments including the fort wall and gives monument-level plan and architectural descriptions. These include the T-shaped Hindola Mahal; Jahaz Mahal between Munj and Kapur tanks; courtyard and mosque complexes; Jami Masjid; and multi-stage structures such as Rupmati's Pavilion. Madhya Pradesh Tourism describes Mandu as a fortified city, while the Dhar district page establishes the present locality and surrounding villages. These sources support modeling a city/plateau system with monuments, water bodies, walls and gateways, but the exact wall trace and gate inventory still need checking against the ASI plan and aerial evidence. Available public descriptions disagree on gate counts, so no count is hard-coded in this review.

The first geometry pass replaces Mandu's single-gate/shelter scene with named monument footprints, the paired Munj/Kapur tanks at Jahaz Mahal, additional water bodies, and the documented northern Alamgiri–Bhangi–Delhi gate sequence. It corrects the scenario to c. 1510 so the Rewa Kund palace group dated by ASI to 1500–1510 is not implied to be finished in 1500. The present-day state now retains this early palace core and Rewa Kund. A new c. 1540 state records ASI/NMA's 1534–1561 attribution of additions to the Rewa Kund group and Rupmati's Pavilion; these changes are not drawn as guessed footprints. Positions and dimensions remain diagrammatic. The full ASI wall/plateau survey, its gate locations and current terrain have not been georeferenced, so this remains explicitly unverified.

The existing Mandu scene is explicitly an encounter-scale local region, not the full fortified plateau or city. Madhya Pradesh Tourism reports a 45 km parapet and 12 gates, and describes the present locality as a village with homes, open-front shops and surrounding fields. The current scene now includes a small village cue only in its present-day state; its placement is illustrative. It still does not model the full 75–82 sq km fortified plateau, full wall circuit or evolving settlement.

The 2025 ASI/National Monuments Authority draft heritage by-laws sharpen the required period model: they place Hoshang Shah's fortifications, Jama Masjid and Hoshang Shah's Tomb in 1405–1435; Jama Masjid completion, Tripolia Gate and Ashrafi Mahal/Madrasa under Mahmud Shah Khilji I (1436–1469); Jahaz Mahal and Hindola Mahal under Ghiyasuddin Khilji (1469–1500); and the Rewa Kund palace under Nasiruddin Khilji (1500–1510). Later additions are assigned to Baz Bahadur's period (1534–1561), Mughal rule (1561–1734), and the Puwars/Marathas (1734–1948). The by-laws point to an ASI archival plan in Annexure II and include a satellite map of protected monuments, but neither has yet been registered to coordinates or checked against dated current imagery. The existing c. 1510 / c. 1540 / today snapshots therefore cover only selected phases and cannot be treated as a complete period reconstruction.

## Chittorgarh: substantial scene, still not a verified reconstruction

Chittorgarh is a useful contrast: it already has a hand-authored ramp and monument district. This pass expands its terrain and perimeter walls to the full approximate 5.6 km north-south outline encoded in the research grid. Its research catalogue includes the UNESCO property map, the 1883–84 ASI report and historic views, photographs and siege imagery. The outline length now fits the stated grid scale, but its lateral coordinates and alignment against UNESCO/ASI plans remain unchecked; most monuments remain in the central district, and settlement, water, and current terrain need fuller mapping. This is an extent correction, not a verified survey reconstruction. Its towers and other prominent structures still need a feature-by-feature match to historic views and current imagery.

The 31 October 2026 aerial review shows the mesa as a long north–south landform immediately beside Chittorgarh's continuous lower-city fabric, with cultivated land outside the urban edge. It supports the city-fort relationship and broad orientation only. The 5.6 km research outline is not registered to the UNESCO property map; gate placements, plateau rim, relief, water features and the transition from town to agricultural land remain unverified. The aerial tile capture date is not displayed.

## Next evidence work before rebuilding these scenes

1. Georeference the UNESCO property/buffer maps, JDA contour and watercourse sheets, the Nahargarh government boundary plan, and available ASI plans to a common coordinate base. Preserve each source's scale, date, control points and uncertainty.
2. Compare those layers with dated current aerial imagery and available ground photographs. Record imagery provider and capture date; do not interpret modern roofs, restoration, or vegetation as historic fabric.
3. Assemble architectural chronology and ownership for each modeled phase, separating surviving original fabric from later repairs, additions, reconstructions, and unknowns.
4. Build the shared region as a connected geography. Use local encounter/battle spaces within it; retain the true city, plateau and ridge outlines.
5. Reconcile geometry against plan, aerial and ground views before calling any of these four forts complete.

## Follow-up: dated phases and living settlement, 31 October 2026

The 2025 ASI/National Monuments Authority draft heritage by-laws for Mandu includes monument survey plans in Annexure I and archival monument maps in Annexure III, in addition to its satellite overview and dated construction table. The current Mandu model is still unregistered to those drawings, but its timeline now withholds later construction from earlier snapshots: the c. 1420 view contains only documented early-phase buildings; the 1454 view adds the Khilji-period groups; and the c. 1480 and c. 1510 views add the 1469–1500 and 1500–1510 groups respectively. The sources date the Rewa Kund palace core to 1500–1510 and later additions to 1534–1561. They also document Mughal-period Nahar Jharokha, Hathi Pol and Neelkanth Palace, followed by Puwar/Maratha-period garhis and Barnes Kothi; these phases are now called out as absent from the model rather than implied to be represented. This is a chronology correction, not a measured reconstruction: the early hall/upper-level succession at Rupmati’s Pavilion and later additions are not geometrically separated, nor are the plan sheets georeferenced.

The Government of Rajasthan/JDA 2023 Nahargarh ESZ report provides stronger evidence for Jaipur’s inhabited context than the earlier schematic patches: it reports a 2022 primary survey of Old Amer over 74.17 ha, including 19.35 ha residential, 0.97 ha commercial, 2.18 ha mixed use and 1.45 ha water body, and includes a 2022 digital elevation model plus road, village, water-body and transport maps. Its regional map also names a connected set of forts, temples, the old Amer town rampart, Panna Meena Kund, gardens and lakes. These are suitable inputs for a connected landscape layer, but the current Amer, Jaigarh and Nahargarh scene patches are far too small and unregistered to be treated as that surveyed geography. The next geometry task is to digitize and register the official map sheets and DEM into a common coordinate frame, then treat the three playable encounters as local views inside that shared relief and settlement.

The 2023 report’s current land-use survey is not evidence of a historical settlement footprint. It should only inform a present-day layer; earlier Jaipur-region states need dated city maps, gazetteers and images. Likewise, the 2022 report map is not a measured wall plan for Amer, Jaigarh or Nahargarh, and its proposed roads or water-retention works should not be depicted as existing historic features.

Current aerial visual review on 31 October 2026 confirms the macro relationship: Amer/Jaigarh sit in the Aravalli ridge landscape northeast of Jaipur; Nahargarh stands on the western ridge above the continuous urban fabric; the Amber–Amer valley retains a village/tourism settlement separate from the dense city. The displayed map attribution is ©2026 Airbus, Landsat/Copernicus, Maxar and Google, but the capture date of the imagery tiles is not displayed. At Mandu, the displayed aerial shows a narrow north–south built-up corridor along the roads, with agricultural parcels to the west and wooded/steep edges to the south and east; the 200 m scale bar spans only the local encounter area, not the wider plateau. These are visual checks of present relationships, not orthophoto registration or evidence about historical footprints.

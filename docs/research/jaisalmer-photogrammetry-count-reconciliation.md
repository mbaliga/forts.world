# Jaisalmer photogrammetry and bastion-count reconciliation

## Findings

Jaisalmer’s hand-authored living-fort scene currently uses a 99-bastion pattern around a schematic outline. That count is not a verified geometry source. The 2013 State Party nomination says the fort has 99 bastions and claims 92 were built or rebuilt between 1633 and 1647. An ASI Jodhpur Circle monument account reports 89; the World Monuments Fund’s 2001 technical mission likewise says its visual survey counted 89, rather than the then-universal claim of 99. The 2001 mission also cautions that the hill-height figure was disputed and says a recent accurate fort plan could not be found at that time.

The 2024 State Party report submitted to UNESCO says that a detailed photogrammetric survey plan defining the core and buffer zones had been prepared. UNESCO’s 2025 decision welcomes completion of the survey. The public report and decision establish that this newer survey exists, but the survey drawing, orthophoto, point cloud, and bastion-level inventory were not found attached to the linked public report. Consequently they cannot yet be used to trace or count the current ramparts.

## Scene decision

Do not move the Jaisalmer outline, rotate its north–south axis, or choose between 89 and 99 from prose alone. The 8 ha UNESCO property area and 11.28 ha ASI account refer to different or insufficiently reconciled site definitions; neither is a control polygon for the modeled fort. The current repeated bastion geometry remains a visible schematic, explicitly identified in the scene as unverified. The historical 1650 snapshot no longer asserts that its bastions are newly completed or individually dated.

The scene source and source catalogue now carry the count conflict and the newer survey lead. The fidelity audit records the missing survey deliverable as the prerequisite for a real footprint correction. Next, obtain the georeferenced photogrammetric plan/orthomosaic and any bastion inventory from ASI, the National Monument Authority, or the Jaisalmer Fort Management Authority; fit the outer and inner walls, gates, bastions, inhabited parcels, and historic approaches to the same control; then compare that dated survey with current aerial imagery and earlier photographs.

## Gatehouse correction

The UNESCO-hosted Site Management Plan describes Akhey Prole as a wooden doorway between two semicircular bastions with a single-storey structure and jharokhas above. The earlier scene used only an open pavilion over the gateway, so it now has a solid one-storey jharokha-gallery mass. Its width, depth, exact façade, door profile and bastion geometry remain schematic because the text provides no measurements. The plan dates the gate to the mid-17th century; the older ASI inventory places its construction under Akshai Singh (1722–1762). The 1650 scene retains the gate based on the Government plan but explicitly flags this unresolved dating disagreement.

## Well relationships from the site management plan

The [UNESCO-hosted Jaisalmer Site Management Plan](https://whc.unesco.org/document/179952), printed pp. 55–57, places Ranisar well about ten feet from Harjaloo and says Ramdeora is a few yards from Khuniwala. The scene previously put each pair far apart. Their schematic relative placements now reflect these reported relationships, with 3.05 m used for the first pair and a provisional 3 m for the second; neither establishes real surveyed coordinates, orientation, or dimensions. The plan additionally locates Jaisaloo in front of Annapurna Temple, but the scene does not yet have an independently registered temple or well position.

The plan attributes Ranisar well to the mid-19th century. The 1650 scene no longer displays that well or its information marker; its precise date and attribution still need verification. The water-system information point no longer claims that all seven documented wells existed in 1650. This does not establish the dates of the other wells. The cited plan includes a well-location map, but its drawing could not be inspected in this pass; no map registration or absolute well placement is claimed. The older fort terrain, architecture, settlement, wall and gate limitations remain.

# Evidence-led geographic views between forts

Updated 2026-10-31. The standalone fort atlas now includes named, source-linked regional views. They are research lenses over the existing generalized regional map—not period-correct basemaps and not traced route geometry. Select a view in **Evidence-led map view** in the standalone fort map to focus its region and read the source argument and limits.

## Evidence-backed world filling for IN-RTS

Fort records are anchors in a larger inhabited landscape. As the map is built out, attach each landscape element to a date range, geographic extent, source locator and evidence state:

1. **Physical frame:** coastline, elevation, slope, watersheds, rivers and seasonal water. Use modern elevation/hydrography only as explicitly modern geographic scaffolding until dated sources or archaeological evidence support a historical change.
2. **Movement frame:** named roads, passes, ferries, bridges, river crossings, ports and caravan routes. Distinguish a source-mapped route from a proposed route corridor; keep route alternatives when a map or text records them.
3. **Settlement and subsistence frame:** towns, villages, markets, wells, tanks, sarais, cultivated zones, grazing, forests and supply stops. Absence from a map is not evidence of absence.
4. **Fort-system frame:** connected forts, passes, ports, rivers and regional capitals, with date-specific control/relationship. Do not connect every pair of nearby forts with an implied road; travel cost should follow terrain, water crossings, season, transport and source evidence.
5. **Campaign frame:** render the selected culture's own map conventions and names as a dated presentation layer over shared geography. Preserve the original map's orientation, hierarchy and uncertainty where the evidence supports it; do not force every historical map into a modern north-up political outline.

### Evidence states for generated fills

- **Observed/documented:** directly visible or stated in a cited map, record, plan or archaeological report.
- **Georeferenced:** source feature registered to shared coordinates, with control points, residual error and transformation method recorded.
- **Inferred corridor:** likely connection between documented nodes; shown as a band or route choice, never as a surveyed road.
- **Procedural interpolation:** generated terrain or land cover between observations; deterministic by seed and visibly marked as game adaptation.
- **Unknown/disputed:** left open, or shown as competing alternatives.

Procedural terrain can supply playable variation inside a geographic envelope. It must not move known rivers, passes, coastlines, forts or documented settlements, and should not silently convert an inferred route into a historical fact. Large-scale travel resolves through regional turns; tactical maps load only for selected local encounters.

## Current implementation boundary

The Atlas of Empires and in-game overview now read a regional 0.25° elevation grid sampled from Mapzen Terrarium z7 tiles. Tile metadata reports SRTM, GMTED and ETOPO1; the mixed products vary in native resolution and vertical reference. This grid represents broad relief at strategic travel scale, not fort contours or historical terrain. Deterministic visual texture and the terrain classes still provide gameplay styling and movement rules. Natural Earth river linework remains modern generalized hydrography, and seasonal crossing delays remain explicit game rules. The two route modes are still inferred travel models rather than reconstructed historic roads. The Survey of India Physical Map of India (2019, 1:4,000,000) remains a comparison reference for broad Indian landforms; it has not been georeferenced into the model. The terrain route report names generalized Natural Earth river lines crossed by its path; the selected dry or monsoon profile changes crossing friction. Those river lines are modern generalized hydrography, while seasonal crossing delay is an explicit game rule and does not establish historical ford conditions. The second route mode follows the authored Konkan/Deccan, Goa/Kanara/Malabar, or eastern Deccan/Tamil coast waypoint graph when both selected places belong to that graph. It applies the scenario data's sea closure and seasonal movement rules and shows the intermediate named nodes. The links are useful campaign topology and retain their own uncertainty; they are not independently verified historic roads. Mixed mode permits sea legs in either route mode. In the main IN-RTS client, a planned route can be started as a saved marching column, advanced one strategic week at a time, and continued after reopening the map; arrival at a playable destination opens its local scene. This first travel loop does not simulate encounters, supply consumption or resupply, hostile territory, or changing weather during the journey. These are gameplay estimates, not source-mapped routes or date-specific hydrology. The campaign datasets supply dated town and port waypoints, their position-confidence fields, and scenario links; waypoint phases are filtered by campaign year. Three additional anchors for the ca. 1760 Mughal Nahr-i Bahisht map record Karnal, the Bhu Ali Qalandar mausoleum at Panipat, and Benawas near Khizrabad. The first two use modern city-centre coordinates; Benawas uses only a broad nearby Khizrabad village anchor. These positions are explicitly separate from the historical feature claims and display their confidence on selection. The layer uses a coarse composite elevation grid, but not fort-scale contours, surveyed pass profiles, seasonal floodplain changes, verified historic roads, a broad settlement gazetteer, or culture-specific cartography.

The evidence-view picker still presents source-linked focus areas with supported-claim and limitation notes. The c. 1770–80 (probable) Delhi–Kandahar scroll now has a playable schematic: Shahjahanabad → Lahore → Kabul → Kandahar. The scholarly description supports that order and notes route-side towns, forts, sarays, branches, villages, rivers, hills, mountains, vegetation and kos minars. The atlas computes separate least-cost legs between approximate modern anchors and draws them as a dotted line. This geometry is inferred and is not georeferenced from the scroll. Kandahar uses a modern city coordinate, not a claimed fort location. Source description and limitations remain visible in the route view.

A second evidence view records the Mughal Nahr-i Bahisht canal map, described as circa 1760. Its source description supports a network of named towns and villages, administrative jurisdictions, distances from the canal, gardens, notable buildings, bridges, distributaries and Persian wheels. The atlas adds Karnal and the Bhu Ali Qalandar mausoleum at Panipat as campaign-year-filtered place records using modern city anchors, plus Benawas near Khizrabad at a broad regional anchor. It does not claim these coordinates are registered from the scroll. The view now adds a source-backed relationship register: Benawas near Khizrabad as the map origin, the approach toward Shahjahanabad, two sarais placed by the thesis just beyond the canal’s entry through the city ramparts, and Delhi Gate as its terminus. It also records source-described canal-side infrastructure and gardens. These records have no coordinates and are displayed only in a clearly labeled schematic; no extra village points or canal trace are asserted. The canal's earlier Tughluq origin and Mughal restoration/extension belong to different historical phases; the source does not establish the map's purpose. This view is a research and design prompt for a source-georeferencing pass, not runtime hydrology.

The Hyderabad evidence view and cultural-heritage layer now distinguish Golconda and Naya Qila from the Qutb Shahi tomb complex, the 1591 Hyderabad foundation around Charminar, and the separate Sultan Nagar/Qila-yi Kohna fort-palace project begun in 1620 and left unfinished. Sultan Nagar has an approximate modern mapped point, not a traced perimeter. The literature documents the wider city walls and gates, but their dated alignments are not yet registered in the project.

The strategic grid is implemented in `src/game/strategic-routing.js`; the landform classes shared with the local campaign are in `src/game/strategic-terrain.js`. Campaign waypoint routing is implemented in `src/game/campaign-network-routing.js`; its edge inputs are the regional `ROUTES` arrays in `konkan.js` and `south.js`. The fort atlas exposes both route models and renders them as distinct inferred paths. The documented Mughal sequence and its separate anchor grades are in `src/data/historical-corridors.js`. The local 6 km campaign hexes use those same broad landform classes for movement costs.

The atlas also has a waypoint-coverage diagnostic. In the current Smith–Newton supplementary table, 3,697 of 3,863 rows have coordinates. Of those coordinate-bearing source rows, 2,427 lie more than 100 km from the 74 currently routeable non-fort anchors: campaign towns/ports/passes, heritage places and historic settlement points. Distance is straight-line approximate kilometres; the comparison is to the current project waypoint set, not to an exhaustive historical settlement inventory. The nearest-point calculation is approximate and includes several city-level anchors and broad landscape points. Smith–Newton rows can repeat place names and their coordinate confidence varies. The standalone atlas coverage overlay remains a research-prioritization cue, not a claim that the marked landscapes lacked settlements. The Godot South Asia map now has a separate optional overlay for all 3,863 source rows (3,697 located); clicks expose the row-level attribution and coordinate caveat rather than silently upgrading them to verified forts. Search and cycle controls make dense clusters and duplicate names inspectable without introducing inferred links between nearby points. Source dataset and limitations: [Smith & Newton (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40537-024-00962-1).
